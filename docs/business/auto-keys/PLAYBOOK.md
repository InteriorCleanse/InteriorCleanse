# Automotive key business: start-to-finish playbook

A mobile automotive locksmith business that cuts keys, programs transponder
keys, remotes, and smart keys, and solves all-keys-lost jobs at the vehicle.
Every step below is something the owner does. Boxes are there to tick.

Checked against sources on 2026-10-01. Licensing and NASTF terms change, so
re-check the linked pages before you rely on a date or a fee.

---

## What this business can and cannot promise

You can cover most of the cars on US roads with one good programmer, one key
machine, and NASTF access. You cannot honestly promise "any vehicle". A
shrinking set of makes, mostly late-model European and some EVs, is
dealer-only or needs online OEM sessions you may not qualify for.
`KEY_REFERENCE.md` lists them. Say "most makes and models" in your
marketing, and keep a dealer referral list for the rest.

## Phase 1. Decide and form the company (week 1)

### 1.1 Name and identity
- [ ] Pick a name that says "car keys" and your city. Search it in your
      state's business registry, on the USPTO trademark search, and as a
      `.com`.
- [ ] Buy the domain and claim the same handle on Facebook and Instagram.
- [ ] Get a dedicated business phone number. Customers call; they rarely
      book online in this trade.

### 1.2 Legal entity and tax
- [ ] Form an LLC with your state's Secretary of State.
- [ ] Get an EIN from the IRS, free, at irs.gov.
- [ ] Open a business bank account and a business card. Never mix funds.
- [ ] Register for state sales tax if your state taxes keys and parts.
      Most do.
- [ ] Get a city or county business license. NASTF needs one dated
      within the last 12 months.

## Phase 2. Licences, insurance, credentials (weeks 1 to 12)

Start NASTF early. Its vetting takes weeks, and without it you cannot pull
key codes or PINs from manufacturers.

### 2.1 Insurance
- [ ] **Commercial general liability, $1,000,000**, with NASTF named as
      additional insured. NASTF requires the certificate.
- [ ] **Garagekeepers coverage.** It pays if you damage a customer's car,
      for example a bricked immobilizer or body control module.
- [ ] **Commercial auto** on the work van.
- [ ] **Inland marine or tools coverage** for the programmer and key
      machine. They are the business.
- [ ] A **surety bond** if your state licence requires one.

### 2.2 State locksmith licence
As of 2026, these states license locksmiths statewide or through county
sheriffs: Alabama, California, Connecticut, Illinois, Louisiana, Maryland,
Nevada, New Jersey, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Oregon, Texas, Virginia.
Florida dropped its rule in July 2025. New York City, Nassau County,
Miami, and Hillsborough County have local rules.

- [ ] Look up your state's board. Most require fingerprints, a background
      check, a fee, and sometimes an exam or apprenticeship hours.
- [ ] Get the licence before your first paid job if your state requires one.

### 2.3 NASTF Vehicle Security Professional registration
This gives you a Locksmith ID, called an LSID. Manufacturers' secure
systems ask for it before they release a key code or immobilizer PIN.

- [ ] Gather the documents: driver's licence, business licence from the
      last 12 months, locksmith licence if your state has one, the $1M
      liability certificate naming NASTF, and proof you own the business.
- [ ] Apply at nastf.org. The fee is about $425 every two years.
- [ ] Pass the background check. Expect 4 to 12 weeks.
- [ ] Read and keep the user agreement. It sets how you verify ownership
      and how long you keep records.
- [ ] Once approved, open an account on each manufacturer service site you
      will use: Ford, GM, Stellantis, Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Hyundai, Kia,
      and others. Each charges per code or per day.
- [ ] For 2018 and newer Stellantis vehicles, set up AutoAuth. Their
      security gateway blocks key programming without it.

### 2.4 Optional but worth it
- [ ] Join ALOA, the Associated Locksmiths of America. Its classes and
      certifications help with insurance, credibility, and states that
      want training hours.

## Phase 3. Tools and training (weeks 2 to 10)

Prices are approximate 2026 street prices. Check current listings before
buying, and buy from authorised dealers so updates and support work.

### 3.1 Starter kit

| Item | Why | Examples | Approx. cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Key programmer | Adds keys through the OBD port, reads PINs on many makes | Autel IM508S or IM608, Xhorse Key Tool Plus, Advanced Diagnostics Smart Pro | $1,500 to $5,000 |
| Programmer updates | Coverage for new model years | Annual subscription per tool | $500 to $1,500 a year |
| Key cutting machine | Cuts by code, high-security and edge cut | Xhorse Condor XC-Mini Plus II, Xhorse Dolphin XP-005, Ilco Futura | $1,000 to $4,000 |
| Transponder cloner | Copies many older keys in seconds | Xhorse Key Tool Max, Keyline 884 Decryptor | $200 to $1,500 |
| Lishi 2-in-1 picks | Open and decode a lock when all keys are lost | Sets for HU66, HU100, TOY43, HON66, HU101, CY24, NSN14 and others | $40 to $70 each |
| Battery maintainer | Holds voltage during programming. Low voltage bricks modules | Any 12V maintainer rated 50A or more | $200 to $500 |
| Lockout kit | Air wedges, long-reach tools | Any commercial kit | $100 to $300 |
| Key and remote stock | Blanks, transponder chips, aftermarket and OEM fobs | Start with the top 15 vehicles in your area | $1,500 to $3,000 |
| Work van, shelving, inverter | Mobile shop | Used cargo van | varies |

Rough starter budget, before the van: about $6,000 to $15,000.

### 3.2 Add later, once jobs pay for it
- [ ] EEPROM and MCU programmer for dash and immobilizer module work on
      all-keys-lost jobs: Xhorse VVDI Prog, Autel XP400 Pro.
- [ ] A second programmer brand. No single tool covers everything.
- [ ] Ignition repair and decoding tools.
- [ ] Dealer software subscriptions for makes you see often.

### 3.3 Training path
- [ ] Take the vendor's training for your programmer. Most offer free
      online courses.
- [ ] Take an ALOA or regional automotive locksmith class.
- [ ] Practise on salvage-yard parts first: ignition cylinders, door locks,
      instrument clusters, and immobilizer modules. Never learn on a
      customer's car.
- [ ] Do 20 practice decodes with each Lishi pick before using it on a job.

## Phase 4. Prices and job types (week 8)

No prices are set in this plan. The owner sets them with this method.

### 4.1 The job menu

| Job | What it means | Harder because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duplicate | Customer has a working key, wants a spare | Easiest, often clone or OBD add |
| Remote only | Replace or add a lock/unlock fob | Programming only |
| Smart key add | Push-to-start fob, customer has one | Pricier stock, sometimes PIN |
| All keys lost | No working key at all | Code retrieval, decode, sometimes module work |
| Lockout | Keys locked inside | Speed matters more than skill |
| Ignition repair | Stuck or worn cylinder | Parts and labour |

### 4.2 Pricing method
1. Call three local mobile locksmiths and ask for a price on the same three
   jobs: a 2015 Toyota Camry spare, a 2018 Honda Civic smart key, and a
   2016 Ford F-150 all-keys-lost. Write the answers down.
2. Call the nearest dealer for the same three. Dealers usually cost more
   and need a tow on all-keys-lost.
3. Work out your floor for each job type:
   `key cost + code or PIN fee + tool token + (hours × your hourly rate) + travel`.
4. Set your price at or below the dealer and in the middle of the three
   locksmiths. Add an after-hours surcharge.
5. Write it down in `PRICES.md` in this folder and quote it the same way
   every time. Quote the full price on the phone, including the trip fee.
   Bait-pricing is the industry's worst reputation problem; avoiding it is
   your edge.

## Phase 5. The job, step by step (every job, forever)

### 5.1 On the phone
- [ ] Year, make, model, and whether it is push-to-start.
- [ ] Does any working key exist?
- [ ] Location, and is the car in a safe spot to work?
- [ ] Ask them to have their ID and title or registration ready.
- [ ] Quote the full price. Give an arrival window.

### 5.2 Ownership verification. No exceptions.
- [ ] Photo ID.
- [ ] Title, registration, or insurance card in the same name.
- [ ] The VIN on the document matches the VIN on the dash and door jamb.
- [ ] If the names differ, get written authorisation from the owner, or
      walk away.
- [ ] Photograph the documents and the VIN plate. Store them in the job
      record for as long as the NASTF agreement and your state require.

A locksmith who skips this step gets caught, loses NASTF access, and can be
charged as an accessory to theft. It also protects you if the car turns out
to be stolen.

### 5.3 Identify
- [ ] Decode the VIN on your programmer or a free VIN decoder.
- [ ] Look up the key blank, transponder chip, and programming method in
      the programmer's vehicle menu. Cross-check with `KEY_REFERENCE.md`
      and the Ilco, Keyline, or Strattec application guide.

### 5.4 Get the cut
Pick the first that applies:
1. **Copy** the existing key on the machine.
2. **Code by VIN** from the manufacturer through your NASTF LSID.
3. **Decode** a door lock with a Lishi pick and cut from the reading.
4. **Remove and read** a lock cylinder and look up its code.

### 5.5 Program
- [ ] Connect the battery maintainer first.
- [ ] Connect the security gateway bypass or AutoAuth where needed.
- [ ] Follow the programmer's procedure. Read the PIN or use the code
      from the manufacturer.
- [ ] Program every key the customer wants in one session. Some systems
      erase keys not present.

### 5.6 Test before taking payment
- [ ] Start the engine with each key.
- [ ] Lock, unlock, trunk, and panic on each remote.
- [ ] Clear any fault codes your work set.
- [ ] Show the customer.

### 5.7 Close
- [ ] Invoice with the job, vehicle, VIN, parts, and warranty terms.
- [ ] Take payment by card or tap.
- [ ] Ask for a Google review by text the same day.

### 5.8 Jobs you turn down
- No proof of ownership.
- A repo or impound job without the lender's or yard's written release.
- Any car whose owner does not want you working on it.
- A make on the dealer-only list. Refer them out and keep the goodwill.

## Phase 7. Getting customers (weeks 10 to 16)

### 7.1 Google first
Most calls come from "car key replacement near me".
- [ ] Create a **Google Business Profile**. Google applies extra
      verification to locksmiths because of fraud, often a video showing
      your vehicle, tools, and licence. Have them ready.
- [ ] Use a service-area profile; do not invent a storefront address.
- [ ] Apply for **Google Local Services Ads**. Locksmiths qualify, and the
      "Google Guaranteed" badge requires a background check and insurance.

### 7.2 Business-to-business, the steady work
- [ ] Used-car dealers: they need spare keys for every car they sell.
- [ ] Tow companies and repair shops: all-keys-lost jobs and referrals.
- [ ] Roadside-assistance networks such as Agero, Honk, and Urgent.ly:
      apply as a provider.
- [ ] Rental and fleet operators: monthly key and remote contracts.

### 7.3 Website
Run `/business-build` with `BRIEF.md` once the name is chosen. It builds the
site in its own repository. The page needs: the phone number at the top,
"most makes and models" coverage, service area, the verification policy,
and real photos of your van and work. No stock photos of other people's vans.

## Phase 8. Money

### 8.1 Start-up budget

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| LLC, EIN, business licence | not stated, varies by state |
| State locksmith licence | not stated, varies by state |
| NASTF VSP, two years | about $425 |
| Insurance, first year | not stated, get three quotes |
| Starter tools and stock | about $6,000 to $15,000 |
| Van | not stated |
| Phone, software, website | not stated |

### 8.2 Break-even
Fill in your own numbers. None are assumed here.

```
monthly fixed costs = insurance + phone + software + tool subscriptions
                      + van payment + fuel baseline + your minimum pay
average job margin  = average price − average parts − average code/token fees
jobs to break even  = monthly fixed costs ÷ average job margin
```
