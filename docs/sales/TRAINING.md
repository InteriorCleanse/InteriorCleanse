# Sales and marketing training: twelve weeks

For a solo technical founder selling software and AI systems to wealthy
individuals and the professionals who serve them: family office staff,
wealth advisers, estate managers, private bankers, lawyers, accountants.
Claude Code runs it with you in this repository, one session at a time.

## 1. How the programme works

Forty-five minutes a day, five days a week. Each week has:

- one skill, stated in two sentences, and short reading;
- one drill, which Claude runs as a role-play partner and grades;
- one real-world rep, an actual call, post, or email you send yourself;
- one number you record in `docs/sales/training-log.csv`;
- a Friday self-review with three fixed questions.

Monday to Thursday: ten minutes of reading, twenty-five minutes of drill,
ten minutes of notes. Friday: the real-world rep if not yet done, then the
review. Start any drill with `/sales-drill <id>` or type the prompt given
under the week.

The three Friday questions, every week: What did the buyer actually say,
in their words? What did I do that I would not do again? What is the one
behaviour I carry into next week?

The rule for Claude: in a drill, Claude is the buyer, not your colleague.
It stays in character, gives you the resistance a real buyer gives, and at
the end scores you against the rubric in section 3 with one behavioural
takeaway. It does not praise effort, does not round up, and does not
soften a low score. Encouragement comes from your own numbers moving.

The rule for you: honesty is the method. No false urgency, no invented
scarcity, no pressure after a no. If a buyer is not a fit, say so and
leave the door open. This buyer has seen every trick and pays for the
absence of them. Nothing in this programme is sent by Claude; you press
every button.

## 2. The twelve weeks

### Week 1. The buyer and the ICP

Skill: describe one specific buyer, their week, and the cost of the
problem you solve, in their words rather than yours. You cannot sell to
"high-net-worth" as a category; you sell to a person with a job.

Reading: `docs/sales/LEAD_ENGINE.md`; `docs/BUSINESS_BUILD_PLAN.md`,
Rules section; The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick), chapters 1 to 3.

Drills:
- 1.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 1.1: you are a family office COO. Interview
  me about who I think you are and correct every guess with what your
  week actually looks like. Grade my ICP description on specificity."
- 1.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 1.2: you are a wealth adviser at a boutique
  RIA. I will describe the problem I solve. Stop me every time I use a
  word you would not use, and grade me on buyer language."

Real-world rep: write one paragraph describing your ICP and send it to
one person who serves that market, asking only "what did I get wrong".

Number: count of named, real people who fit the ICP on your list.

Pass bar: an ICP paragraph a real practitioner amended rather than
rejected, and at least twenty named people on the list.

### Week 2. Listening and discovery questions

Skill: ask questions that make the buyer describe their current state,
the cost of it, and what a fixed state is worth, before you say a word
about your product. The best discovery call ends with the buyer having
talked more than you.

Reading: `docs/sales/SCRIPTS.md`, section 4; SPIN Selling (Neil
Rackham), chapters 1 to 4; Gap Selling (Keenan), part 1.

Drills:
- 2.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 2.1: you are an estate manager for a family
  with three properties. I have a thirty-minute discovery call. Grade me
  on the discovery rubric."
- 2.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 2.2: same as 2.1, but you are guarded and
  answer briefly until a question earns a longer answer. Discovery
  rubric, and report my talk ratio."

Real-world rep: one real discovery conversation, even informal, with
someone in the ICP or adjacent. No pitch allowed.

Number: questions asked before first mention of your product.

Pass bar: discovery rubric 14/20 or better on two consecutive drills.

### Week 3. The cold and referral opener

Skill: open a conversation in under twenty seconds by saying who you are,
why this person, and what you are asking for, then stop talking. A
referral opener names the referrer and the specific reason in the first
sentence.

Reading: `docs/sales/SCRIPTS.md`, sections 1 to 3; Let's Get Real or
Let's Not Play (Mahan Khalsa), the chapter on intent.

Drills:
- 3.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 3.1: you are a private banker, I was
  referred by a mutual client. Play the first two minutes and grade my
  opener on clarity, relevance, and ask."
- 3.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 3.2: you are a family office COO, I am
  calling cold, grade me on the rubric."

Real-world rep: send three openers, at least one referred, one cold.

Number: openers sent, and conversations they produced.

Pass bar: an opener under sixty words that Claude, in character, agrees
to hear out in three of four attempts.

### Week 4. Objections

Skill: treat an objection as information about the buyer's world, not an
attack on yours. Acknowledge it in their words, ask what sits under it,
answer only the real thing, and check whether the answer landed.

Reading: `docs/sales/SCRIPTS.md`, objections section; Never Split the
Difference (Chris Voss with Tahl Raz), chapters on labelling and
calibrated questions.

Drills:
- 4.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 4.1: you are a wealth adviser. Raise the
  five objections in SCRIPTS.md one at a time. Grade each response on the
  objection rubric."
- 4.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 4.2: you are a family principal's chief of
  staff. Raise objections I have not prepared for. Objection rubric."

Real-world rep: log every objection you hear this week, verbatim, in
your notes, with what you said back.

Number: objections heard, and how many you asked a question about before
answering.

Pass bar: objection rubric 8/10 or better on five distinct objections.

### Week 5. Pricing and saying a number

Skill: state a price as a plain fact, connected to the value the buyer
described in discovery, then be silent. Discounting before being asked
tells the buyer the first number was not real.

Reading: $100M Offers (Alex Hormozi), the value equation chapters;
`docs/sales/HIGH_VALUE_SERVICES.md`, pricing section.

Drills:
- 5.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 5.1: you are a COO who has finished
  discovery with me. Ask what it costs. Grade whether I anchor to value
  I heard from you, say one number, and stop."
- 5.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 5.2: same buyer, but react to the price with
  silence, then with 'that is more than I expected'. Grade whether I
  hold, explain, or cave."

Real-world rep: say a real price to a real person, aloud or in writing.

Number: prices stated, and how many you reduced without being asked.

Pass bar: five drill repetitions in which the number is said once, with
no unprompted discount.

### Week 6. Proposals and closing

Skill: a proposal restates the buyer's problem in their words, names the
outcome, the price, and the next step, on one page. Closing is asking a
clear question and accepting the answer, including no.

Reading: `docs/sales/HIGH_VALUE_SERVICES.md`, engagement structure;
Let's Get Real or Let's Not Play (Khalsa), on decision process.

Drills:
- 6.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 6.1: I will paste a proposal draft. You are
  the buyer reading it for the first time. Tell me where you stopped
  reading and why, then grade it on the written outreach rubric."
- 6.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 6.2: you are a buyer at the end of a
  proposal review. I will ask for the decision. Grade the ask and how I
  handle a yes, a no, and a maybe."

Real-world rep: send one real proposal with a date for a decision.

Number: proposals sent, and decisions reached either way.

Pass bar: one real proposal sent and one clear yes or no received,
without a follow-up that pressures.

### Week 7. Rapport with high-net-worth buyers and their gatekeepers

Skill: the gatekeeper is a buyer with their own job and their own risk;
respect their time and make them look good for having let you in. With
the principal, be brief, be precise, and never perform.

Reading: `docs/sales/HIGH_VALUE_SERVICES.md`, section 4; Influence
(Robert Cialdini), the reciprocity and consistency chapters, read as a
description of what to notice, not what to do.

Drills:
- 7.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 7.1: you are an executive assistant to a
  family principal. I want fifteen minutes with your principal. Grade me
  on whether I treat you as a decision-maker."
- 7.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 7.2: you are the principal, impatient and
  already advised by three professionals. I have ten minutes. Grade on
  brevity, precision, and whether I ask before I tell."

Real-world rep: one conversation with a gatekeeper where you ask what
would make their job easier, and act on it.

Number: introductions requested, and introductions received.

Pass bar: two gatekeeper drills in which Claude, in character, chooses
to make the introduction.

### Week 8. Written outreach and LinkedIn presence

Skill: a written message is judged in the first line and answered
because of the last one. Your profile should read as a specialist a
professional would refer to a client without embarrassment.

Reading: Ogilvy on Advertising (David Ogilvy), the chapter on writing
copy; `docs/sales/SCRIPTS.md`, written outreach section.

Drills:
- 8.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 8.1: I will paste three outreach messages.
  Read each as a busy adviser and grade on the written outreach rubric."
- 8.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 8.2: read my LinkedIn headline and about
  section as a family office COO deciding whether to accept a connection.
  Tell me what you conclude in ten seconds, then grade the copy."

Real-world rep: rewrite your headline and about section; send five
outreach messages.

Number: messages sent, replies received.

Pass bar: written rubric 8/10 on three messages, profile rewritten.

### Week 9. Content marketing

Skill: content earns attention by being useful before it asks for
anything. The faceless scripts show the form: one idea, one proof, one
step. Your build-in-public posts show your work as it happens, without
claims you cannot show.

Reading: `content/marketing/faceless-video-scripts.md`, the cadence and
"Before you post" sections; Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale",
paulgraham.com/ds.html.

Drills:
- 9.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 9.1: I will paste a build-in-public post.
  You are a sceptical adviser who reads it. Say what you learned, what
  you doubt, and grade on the written outreach rubric adapted for posts."
- 9.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 9.2: give me one problem my ICP has. I have
  ten minutes to draft a post that teaches something and shows my work.
  Grade specificity and honesty."

Real-world rep: publish three posts, each showing one real thing you
built or learned.

Number: posts published, and replies from people in the ICP.

Pass bar: three posts live, none containing a claim you cannot show.

### Week 10. Email marketing

Skill: an email sequence is a conversation spread over time; each email
earns the next one. The existing launch sequence shows the structure:
one purpose per email, plain subject lines, no send you would not want
forwarded.

Reading: `content/marketing/email-launch-sequence.md`, sections 1 and 2;
Ogilvy on Advertising, on direct response.

Drills:
- 10.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 10.1: I will paste a five-email sequence
  for an ICP list. Read each as a recipient. Say which you would delete
  unread and why, and grade each on the written outreach rubric."
- 10.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 10.2: give me the subject lines from
  email-launch-sequence.md. I will write five for my own list. Grade
  them on clarity and honesty."

Real-world rep: send one real email to your list, however small.

Number: emails sent, replies received.

Pass bar: a sequence of five drafted, each scoring 8/10, one sent.

### Week 11. Pipeline maths and forecasting

Skill: a pipeline is a list of named deals with evidence at each stage,
not a feeling. Forecasting is stating what has to be true for a deal to
close and showing the evidence that each condition is met.

Reading: `docs/sales/LEAD_ENGINE.md`, pipeline section; Predictable
Revenue (Aaron Ross and Marylou Tyler), the chapters on funnel stages.

Drills:
- 11.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 11.1: I will paste my pipeline. You are my
  sales manager. For each deal ask what we do not know, and rate each
  as upside, commit, or dead with a reason."
- 11.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 11.2: using my recorded numbers from weeks
  3 to 10, work out my conversion at each stage and how many openers a
  month produce one signed client. Show the arithmetic."

Real-world rep: write your own forecast for the next thirty days with
the evidence for each committed deal.

Number: pipeline coverage: qualified pipeline value divided by your
monthly revenue target.

Pass bar: a forecast with evidence per deal, and your own conversion
ratios computed from real numbers.

### Week 12. Negotiation, contracts, and account expansion

Skill: negotiation is discovering what each side values and trading
across it, not splitting a number. Expansion is earned by delivering
what was promised and asking, at the right moment, what else is costing
the client.

Reading: Getting to Yes (Roger Fisher and William Ury), chapters on
interests and options; Never Split the Difference, on calibrated
questions; `docs/sales/HIGH_VALUE_SERVICES.md`, terms section.

Drills:
- 12.1 (Mon, Tue): "Run drill 12.1: you are a family office general
  counsel negotiating my terms. Push on price, liability, and
  termination. Grade whether I trade rather than concede, and whether I
  hold anything I said was fixed."
- 12.2 (Wed, Thu): "Run drill 12.2: you are a client at the end of a
  successful first engagement. I will run an expansion conversation.
  Discovery rubric, plus whether I earn the ask."

Real-world rep: one contract sent with terms you can explain, or one
expansion conversation with an existing client.

Number: contracts signed, expansion conversations held.

Pass bar: a negotiation drill with no unplanned concession, and one real
contract or expansion conversation.

After week 12: repeat weeks 2, 4, and 5 in rotation while the
real-world reps continue. The programme ends; the drills do not.

## 3. Grading rubrics

Claude scores each criterion 0, 1, or 2 and reports the total, the
lowest-scoring criterion, and one behavioural change for next time.

### Discovery call (out of 20)

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening intent | Launched into pitch | Stated purpose vaguely | Stated purpose, agenda, and asked permission |
| Current state | Assumed the buyer's situation | Asked, accepted first answer | Asked and followed up until specific |
| Cost of the problem | Never asked | Asked, got a vague answer | Buyer named a number or a concrete consequence |
| Implication | Jumped to solution | One implication question | Buyer described what happens if unchanged |
| Desired state | Seller described it | Asked once | Buyer described it in their own words |
| Talk ratio | Seller spoke most | Roughly even | Buyer spoke clearly more |
| Follow-up depth | Changed topic after each answer | One follow-up | Two or more follow-ups on the important answer |
| Decision process | Not asked | Asked who decides | Asked who, how, when, and what criteria |
| Silence and pace | Filled every pause | Some rushing | Let silence work; buyer kept talking |
| Next step | None or "I'll follow up" | Proposed a step | Agreed specific step, date, and purpose |

### Objection response (out of 10)

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge | Ignored or argued | Generic "I understand" | Restated in buyer's words |
| Question beneath | Answered immediately | One shallow question | Asked what sits under it and listened |
| Honest answer | Deflected or overclaimed | Partly true, partly spin | Answered the real thing, including limits |
| Proof | None | Asserted | Offered evidence the buyer could check |
| Check | Moved on | "Does that help?" | Asked whether it changed anything, and accepted the answer |

### Written outreach (out of 10)

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| First line | About the sender | Generic relevance | Specific to this reader, visibly researched |
| Length | Over 150 words | 100 to 150 | Under 100, nothing wasted |
| Value before ask | Ask only | Weak value | Something useful whether or not they reply |
| Honesty | Any invented figure or urgency | Vague claims | Only what you can show |
| The ask | None or several | One, vague | One, small, easy to answer |

## 4. Source material

- SPIN Selling, Neil Rackham. Take the four-question sequence and the
  discipline of asking before presenting. Ignore the large-account
  corporate context; adapt the questions to a one-hour call.
- The Challenger Sale, Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson. Take teaching
  the buyer something about their own situation. Ignore "taking control"
  and manufactured tension; this buyer leaves the room.
- Never Split the Difference, Chris Voss with Tahl Raz. Take labelling,
  mirroring, calibrated questions, and treating no as safe. Ignore any
  tactic you would not be comfortable telling the buyer you are using.
- Gap Selling, Keenan. Take the current-state, future-state, gap
  structure and quantifying the problem. Ignore the aggression; keep the
  discipline.
- $100M Offers, Alex Hormozi. Take the value equation and guarantee
  design. Ignore the urgency and scarcity stacks; they are banned here.
- Influence, Robert Cialdini. Take the six principles as things to
  recognise in yourself and the buyer. Ignore them as levers to pull;
  the book itself warns against that.
- Ogilvy on Advertising, David Ogilvy. Take headlines, long copy that
  respects the reader, and research before writing. Ignore the
  mass-media economics.
- The Mom Test, Rob Fitzpatrick. Take how to ask about someone's life
  without pitching. Ignore the startup-idea framing.
- Let's Get Real or Let's Not Play, Mahan Khalsa. Take intent, decision
  process, and walking away honestly. Ignore the consulting-firm scale.
- Getting to Yes, Roger Fisher and William Ury. Take interests over
  positions and inventing options. Ignore anything that assumes equal
  power; you have less.
- Predictable Revenue, Aaron Ross and Marylou Tyler. Take stage
  definitions and specialisation. Ignore the team-of-SDRs model; you are
  one person.
- "Do Things That Don't Scale", Paul Graham, paulgraham.com/ds.html.
  Take manual recruitment of early customers. Ignore the venture context.

## 5. The `/sales-drill` command

Defined in `.claude/commands/sales-drill.md`. Argument: a drill id from
this document, such as `3.2`.

1. Read this file, find the week containing the drill, load its skill,
   reading, drill prompt, and pass bar. Load any repo file the week
   cites if it exists; skip missing ones and say so.
2. Set up the role-play: Claude states the buyer it is playing in one
   line, then stays in character until the owner types `end` or
   `score`.
3. Score against the rubric the drill names (discovery, objection, or
   written). Report total, lowest criterion, and one behavioural
   takeaway. No praise of effort.
4. Append one line to `docs/sales/training-log.csv`: today's date, the
   drill id, the score as `n/max`, a note under twelve words.
5. Never send, post, or email anything. The command touches only this
   repository.
